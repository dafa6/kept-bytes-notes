Two notes on the parts of a generation you only miss after they are gone.

Reading a model announcement for what it does not say

A new image model ships. The announcement says it is better. Three weeks later people are arguing about whether it actually is, because "better" was never a claim you could check.

The useful skill here is not scepticism — the models genuinely do improve — it is reading the specific wording, because release posts are written carefully and the care is visible if you look for it.

The documentation is usually more precise than the blog post

This is the single highest-value habit. The announcement is written by marketing and optimises for a headline; the API reference and the model card are written by the people who have to support it and optimise for not being wrong.

When the two disagree in emphasis, the documentation is the one to believe. A release post that says "our new model sets a new standard for image quality" will often sit next to documentation saying that one particular variant beats the previous generation on quality, and the other one trades quality for speed. Those are very different claims, and only one of them is checkable.

Five phrases worth slowing down for

"Up to." A ceiling under ideal conditions. It tells you the best case exists; it tells you nothing about the median. Every instance of "up to" is an invitation to ask "in what configuration".

"Comparable to" / "on par with." Usually means "loses narrowly". Vendors do not use this phrasing about things they win.

"Improved" with no baseline. Improved against what — the previous version, a competitor, an internal build? Without the baseline this is not a measurement.

A benchmark name with no configuration. Arena-style leaderboards are real signal, but the number depends on which variant ran, at which tier, at what size. A score quoted without those is not reproducible, and an incremental Elo gain often gets described in language that suggests a step change.

Silence about a capability. The most informative part of any announcement. If a release discusses quality, speed and price at length and never mentions transparency support, text rendering or multi-turn consistency, that is not an oversight.

The questions that actually decide it for you

Announcements are written for everyone. Your decision is not. Four questions, none of which a release post answers:

  1. What does one image cost at my configuration? Token billing means the published rate is not a price per image. You have to compute it.
  2. Which variant should I use for my job? When two variants cost the same, the choice is entirely about the trade-off, and nobody else can make it for you.
  3. Did the thing that was blocking me get fixed? If you switched away from the previous generation for a specific reason, that reason is the only benchmark that matters.
  4. Can I tell which one served my request? If the interface does not report the variant, everything above is unverifiable in practice.

Why comparisons that concede things are worth more

A comparison page that has its subject winning every category is not telling you about the subject, it is telling you who wrote it. It is also useless as a decision tool, because the decision is precisely about the cases where the answer is not the same.

The ones worth reading name the competitor's wins first and are specific about them. That framing is unusual enough that it is a reasonable proxy for whether the rest of the page was written honestly — and it is the version that gets quoted, because a concession is evidence of something in a way that a claim is not.

Applied to the current generation: the honest summary is that the headline change is the price ladder rather than the quality, and that only one of the two variants beats the previous generation on quality — which is what the vendor's own documentation says, more precisely than the announcement did. Both are set out at gptimage25.top, and the head-to-head against the strongest competitor is a comparison that names the competitor's wins first.
